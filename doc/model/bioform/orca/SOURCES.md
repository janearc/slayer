# Sources for the orca-clean-05 skeleton, teeth and body

The code under manufacture/animals/vertebrates/mammals/whales cites these by number
(S1 and on) and gives no names of people or places itself. Each entry
says the page that was read, the work it cites where it cites one, and
the value as written there. Nothing was downloaded; pages were read in
a browser-like fetch. A number in the code that is not traced to one of
these says in its own comment that it was chosen.

## S1. Killer whale skeleton record, Idaho Virtual Museum

virtual.imnh.iri.isu.edu/Osteo/View/Killer_Whale/717. Specimen
"Juvenile Male Orca Kruzof", courtesy of Sitka Sound Science Center.

Read: views "Cervical Vertebrae 1 through 7", "Thoracic 1 through 10",
"Lumbar 1 through 12", caudal views to 22 before the page text was cut
off; "Rib First (Left)" to "Rib 10 (Left)"; "Sternal Rib 1" to
"Sternal Rib 8"; "Sternum (Axial)"; "Chevron 1" to "Chevron 13
(Axial)"; "Metacarpal 1" to "Metacarpal 5".

Right manus phalanges:
"Digit one, Proximal"; digit two "Proximal", "Medial", "Distal",
"Phalanx 2"; digit three "Proximal", "Medial", "Distal"; digit four
"Proximal", "Distal"; digit five "Proximal". A juvenile; an adult may
differ.

## S2. Vertebral count

Buchholtz, E. A. and Schur, S. A. (2004), "Vertebral osteology in
Delphinidae (Cetacea)", Zoological Journal of the Linnean Society
140(3):383-401, figure 7, read on zenodo.org/record/5429021: "Orcinus
orca, AMNH 34276, total count = 53". One specimen.

## S3. Teeth, fin and size, OBIS-SEAMAP species page

seamap.env.duke.edu/species/180469/html: "There are 10-12 large,
recurved teeth in each half of both jaws, which are oval in cross
section."; dorsal fin "reaching 0.9 m in females and 1.8 m in males";
"Adult females are up to 8.5 m and 7,500 kg".

## S4. Teeth per quadrant

A web search result quoting PMC12315212, which could not be opened

quadrant (Heyning and Dahlheim 1988)", citing Heyning, J. E. and

## S5. Mass and head, Wikipedia's killer whale page

en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Killer_whale: females "weighing about 3 to 4 t",
citing Baird (2002, p. 129); "The snout is blunt and lacks the beak of
other species", citing Heyning (1988).

## S6. Swimming

The biologging observations of Holt and others,
pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC5319153/: vertical motion
concentrated in the posterior third. The swim cycle is an authored
hypothesis, not a fit to recorded kinematics.

## S7. Tailbeat frequency

A study of Southern Resident whales, doi.org/10.1007/s00265-024-03492-1:
tailbeats across 0.3 to 1.28 Hz, mean 0.55 Hz, depending on the
individual and the swimming speed. Used only to judge that a slow cycle
is in range; the shipped cycle is 3 s, about 0.33 Hz, and is chosen.

## S8. Appearance and size, NOAA Fisheries

- Species description, fisheries.noaa.gov/species/killer-whale: black
  back, white underside and eye patches, a pale saddle; markings and
  appendages vary by individual, population and sex; dorsal, pectoral
  and fluke size differ by sex.
- Southwest Region observer field manual (fisheries.noaa.gov/s3/2021-07/
  Southwest-Region-Observer-Program-Field-Manual.pdf): a blunt head,
  almost no beak, a dorsal fin at midlength, paddle-like pectorals.
- Killer whale comparison infographic, illustrated by Uko Gorter
  (fisheries.noaa.gov/s3/2024-03/KillerWhaleComparison-Infographic-
  SWFSC-MMTD-Milstein.pdf): adult resident females at least 21 ft
  (about 6.4 m), a rounded-tip curved dorsal fin, variable open saddle
  patterns. Compared by eye as a side view; nothing was imported.
- Ecotype poster (cd2-www.fisheries.noaa.gov/s3/dam-migration/
  killer_whale_ecotypes_poster_final.pdf): minimal beak, a low rounded
  adult-female dorsal, paddle pectorals, a light post-ocular patch, an
  open gray saddle and a curved ventral white flank blaze. Compared by
  eye as a side view; nothing was imported.

## S9. Morphology, Morin and others (2024)

pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC10966402/: a robust spindle-shaped
body, wide horizontal flukes, a short poorly defined beak, size and
sexual differences. Taxonomy is under revision; the model uses the
familiar Orcinus orca and a resident-like form, with no claim to a
population.
